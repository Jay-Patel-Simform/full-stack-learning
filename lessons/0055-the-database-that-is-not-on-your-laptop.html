Lesson 55 · Week 15–16 · Docker + deploy

The database that is not on your laptop

You asked for the deploy. This is the first half of it, and it is the half that decides whether the second half works. Today your container stays on your Mac, but its database moves to the internet. Two things in your own files only work on this laptop, and you will find both. Zero TypeScript changes. Suite stays at 172.

In one minute

  • The database moves first, the app moves second. A deploy that changes both at once gives you two suspects for every error. Lesson 56 moves the app.
  • A managed Postgres (a database someone else runs and backs up) gives you a URL, not a machine. We use Neon: free, permanent, no credit card, 0.5 GB.
  • You get two URLs, and they are not interchangeable. One goes through a connection pooler (a middleman that shares a few real connections between many callers). One is direct, straight to Postgres. Migrations need the direct one.
  • Your tasks_app.sql has two names in it that exist only here: the database tasks and the role tasks. Two words fix it forever. I ran the fix against your container.
  • Neon refuses the password tasks_app. It demands real entropy. The rotation I have asked for five times is now not my opinion, it is a wall.
  • The new password does not go in the SQL file. That file gets committed in lesson 56, and a secret committed once is in the history forever. psql’s \getenv reads it from the environment instead — and .env.docker, which now holds two real credentials, goes into .gitignore today.
  • Streak intact: no new dependency, 55 lessons.

Why this is not the deploy

I planned lesson 55 as the whole deploy. Then I read your prisma/sql/tasks_app.sql again and found this line:

GRANT CONNECT ON DATABASE tasks TO tasks_app;

On Neon your database is called neondb. That line would fail. Four lines below it there is a second one:

ALTER DEFAULT PRIVILEGES FOR ROLE tasks IN SCHEMA public
  GRANT SELECT, INSERT, UPDATE, DELETE ON TABLES TO tasks_app;

On Neon the owner role is called neondb_owner. That line would succeed and do nothing, because it would describe a role that creates no tables. Your app would boot, read fine, and then fail on the first INSERT into any table a future migration adds.

Move one thing at a time. If the app and the database both moved today, that second failure would arrive next month with a deploy to blame. Today there is only one suspect.

Step 1 — the free database

Go to neon.com, sign up, create a project. No card. Pick the region closest to you. You now have a database called neondb and a role called neondb_owner.

On the project's Connect panel you get two connection strings. They differ by six characters:

pooled   postgresql://neondb_owner:PASS@ep-cool-name-123-pooler.eu-central-1.aws.neon.tech/neondb?sslmode=require
direct   postgresql://neondb_owner:PASS@ep-cool-name-123.eu-central-1.aws.neon.tech/neondb?sslmode=require
URLWhat it is, and what it is for
-poolerGoes through PgBouncer, a connection pooler. Postgres can only hold a few hundred connections at once, and each one costs memory. The pooler holds those and lends them out per query. This is what a web app should use.
no -poolerA real, private connection to Postgres. Slower to open, but it is yours until you close it. Migrations must use this.

Why migrations must use the direct one is the thing you learned last lesson. prisma migrate deploy takes an advisory lock so that two containers cannot migrate at once. A lock belongs to a connection. Through a pooler, your next statement can arrive on a different connection, which does not hold the lock — so the safety net from lesson 54 quietly stops existing. Neon says the same thing, in one line, without the reason.

You already have two URLs, and they already split along this line. DATABASE_URL is the owner, used by migrations. APP_DATABASE_URL is tasks_app, used by every request. Owner → direct. App → pooled. The decision lesson 42 made for security also happens to be the right one for pooling.

Step 2 — the two words that make your SQL portable

Open server/prisma/sql/tasks_app.sql. Change exactly two things:

GRANT CONNECT ON DATABASE :"DBNAME" TO tasks_app;

ALTER DEFAULT PRIVILEGES FOR ROLE :"USER" IN SCHEMA public
  GRANT SELECT, INSERT, UPDATE, DELETE ON TABLES TO tasks_app;
ALTER DEFAULT PRIVILEGES FOR ROLE :"USER" IN SCHEMA public
  GRANT USAGE, SELECT ON SEQUENCES TO tasks_app;

DBNAME and USER are psql automatic variables — values psql fills in from the connection you gave it. You set nothing. :"NAME" with the quotes inside means put this in as an identifier, so a database called my-db would not break the statement.

This is measured, not guessed. I ran it against the container running on your Mac right now:

$ docker compose exec -T db psql -U tasks -d tasks -f - < probe.sql
 whoami |  db
--------+-------
 tasks  | tasks
CREATE ROLE
GRANT
ALTER DEFAULT PRIVILEGES

Same file, two databases, no copy. The alternative was a second SQL file for Neon that drifts out of step with this one by next month.

One caveat, and it is small: :VARIABLES are a psql feature, not a Postgres one. This file is already a psql file — lesson 53 mounts it into docker-entrypoint-initdb.d, which runs psql. Nothing else reads it.

Step 3 — the password, finally

I have asked five times. Neon asks once and means it: a role password needs about 60 bits of entropy, roughly twelve mixed characters. tasks_app is rejected at CREATE ROLE. Generate one:

openssl rand -base64 18 | tr '+/' '-_'

The tr is not decoration. Base64 emits + and /, and this password has to survive being pasted into the middle of a URL, where / ends the host and + can mean a space. Swapping them for - and _ costs no entropy and saves you an hour of reading a connection error that says nothing useful.

That is 144 bits, and openssl is already on your Mac. Now stop before you paste it, because the obvious place is the wrong one.

The obvious place is a file you are about to commit

The line as written today is this:

CREATE ROLE tasks_app LOGIN PASSWORD 'tasks_app';

Replace tasks_app with a real secret and prisma/sql/tasks_app.sql becomes a file that carries a live credential. That file is source. It is mounted by compose, it belongs next to the schema, and lesson 56 puts this directory under git. A secret committed once is in the history forever — deleting the line later does not remove it, it only removes it from the tip. Rotating is then the only real fix, and you rotate by doing this whole step again.

So the password never goes in the file. psql can read it from the environment at run time:

\getenv app_password APP_PASSWORD
CREATE ROLE tasks_app LOGIN PASSWORD :'app_password';

\getenv var ENVNAME is a psql backslash command: it copies an environment variable into a psql variable. :'name' — quotes outside the colon — means put this in as a string literal, correctly escaped. Compare it with the :"DBNAME" from step 1: quotes inside is an identifier, quotes outside is a literal. A password is a literal. A database name is an identifier. The two forms are not interchangeable and psql will not guess for you.

Same trick as step 1, one rung further. There, a value that was true only on your laptop came out of the file. Here, a value that must never be in any file comes out too. What is left is a file that describes privileges and names no environment and no secret — which is the only kind of file that is safe to commit.

Three places, not two

WhereWhat changes
prisma/sql/tasks_app.sqlthe two lines above — and delete the ! comment about changing the password, because the file can no longer hold one.
.env.dockerthe password inside APP_DATABASE_URL, and a new APP_PASSWORD= line for \getenv to find.
server/compose.ymlenv_file: .env.docker on the db service, so the init run has APP_PASSWORD in its environment.

The compose change is the part that is easy to miss. The db service had no env_file — it set POSTGRES_USER and friends inline and needed nothing else. Now the SQL it runs at init reads an environment variable, so the variable has to be in that container. Add one line:

  db:
    image: postgres:17
    env_file: .env.docker
    environment:
      POSTGRES_USER: tasks

The inline environment: block still wins over env_file for anything named in both, so the Postgres settings are unaffected. The container also picks up DATABASE_URL and the rest, which Postgres ignores.

Your local container database already has the old role. docker compose down -v and let the init file build it fresh — the volume matters much less after today.

And the file that does hold the secret

Check .gitignore at the repo root. Mine had three lines and .env.docker was not one of them:

.neon
.env.local
node_modules/
.env.docker

Moving a secret does not protect it, it relocates it. Everything above pushes the password out of a committed file into .env.docker — which accomplishes nothing at all unless .env.docker is ignored. It is already out of the image (.dockerignore has .env.*, and compose passes it at run time with env_file). Git is the other half, and lesson 56 is the one that introduces a repository. Do it now, while the file is still untracked and the fix costs one line.

Step 4 — run your file against Neon

One command. Use the direct URL, quoted, with the password in front of it as an environment variable — that is what \getenv reads:

cd server
APP_PASSWORD='<new>' psql "postgresql://neondb_owner:PASS@ep-cool-name-123.eu-central-1.aws.neon.tech/neondb?sslmode=require" \
  -f prisma/sql/tasks_app.sql

A variable written in front of a command like that exists only for that command — it is not exported, and it is not in your shell afterwards. It is in your shell history, which is the one hole left; a leading space suppresses that in zsh.

Expect CREATE ROLE, three GRANT, two ALTER DEFAULT PRIVILEGES. The GRANT ... ON ALL TABLES lines will say GRANT while granting nothing, because there are no tables yet. That is correct and is exactly why the ALTER DEFAULT PRIVILEGES lines exist — they cover the tables your migrations are about to create.

If psql is not on your PATH, you have it inside the container: docker compose exec -T db psql "<url>" -f - with the file piped in.

One Neon detail worth knowing before it confuses you: a free database scales to zero after five minutes with no queries. The next query wakes it and takes about half a second. Nothing is lost. It is why the free plan can be permanent.

Step 5 — point the container at it

.env.docker, the whole file. Two URLs change and one line is new, nothing else moves:

DATABASE_URL=postgresql://neondb_owner:OWNERPASS@ep-cool-name-123.eu-central-1.aws.neon.tech/neondb?sslmode=require
APP_DATABASE_URL=postgresql://tasks_app:NEWPASS@ep-cool-name-123-pooler.eu-central-1.aws.neon.tech/neondb?sslmode=require
APP_PASSWORD=NEWPASS
NODE_ENV=development
ALLOWED_ORIGINS=http://localhost:5173
TRUST_PROXY=false
HOST=0.0.0.0
PORT=3000

Read those two lines against each other. Different role, different host, one database. The owner goes in the front door. The internet goes through the pooler and owns nothing.

NODE_ENV and TRUST_PROXY stay as they are. There is still no HTTPS and still no proxy in front of you. Lesson 56 changes both, and being honest about it today is the whole reason lesson 29's cookie bug is not waiting for you.

The failure you should cause on purpose

Swap it: put the -pooler host into DATABASE_URL, rebuild, and watch the migration step. With fifteen migrations already applied you will most likely get away with it — and that is the point worth feeling. A wrong URL here does not fail loudly. It fails the day two containers start at once and both decide they are the one migrating.

Put it back. Then read your .env.docker one more time and check which line has -pooler in it.

Why must prisma migrate deploy use the direct URL?

Prisma takes an advisory lock so two containers cannot migrate at once (lesson 54). A lock belongs to the connection that took it. A pooler can put your next statement on a different connection, so the lock stops protecting you — silently, and only under the exact conditions it existed for.

What does :"USER" in the SQL file do?

It is a psql variable, replaced before Postgres ever sees the statement. psql sets USER and DBNAME from the connection string you gave it. So the same file says tasks on your Mac and neondb_owner on Neon, with nothing for you to remember.

Why move the database before moving the app?

Two of your SQL lines named things that exist only on this laptop, and one of them would have failed silently. Found today, it is a two-word edit. Found during a deploy that also changed the host, the build, the env and the proxy, it is an evening.

What you did not build, on purpose

left out
the deploy itself lesson 56: a Render web service built from your Dockerfile, a git repository, secrets in a dashboard instead of a file, and a real HTTPS URL. Free, no card — it sleeps after 15 minutes idle and takes about a minute to wake.
NODE_ENV=production and TRUST_PROXY=true both are wrong until there is actually a proxy and actually HTTPS. Setting them today would make cookies secure on a plain-HTTP localhost and log you out. Lesson 29 predicted this exact pairing.
deleting the local Postgres npm test still runs against the Mac's native Postgres, and should. Tests that need the internet are tests you stop running.
connection limits and ?connection_limit= Prisma opens a pool of its own, behind Neon's pool. Two pools is one too many to reason about before there is traffic to measure. It becomes real when the app is up and something times out.

That refusal was written on your laptop in lesson 41 and has now survived a move to another continent, which is the best evidence you will get that the rule lives in the database and not in your code.
