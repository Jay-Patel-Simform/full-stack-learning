The role that can erase the evidence

Your audit log has been running since lesson 36. It now holds 6,664 rows in your dev database. Every one of them can be deleted by the same database login your API uses to serve requests. Today you look at the one permission system in this app you have never touched: the database role. I ran the whole experiment on a throwaway copy of your database, so every number below is measured, and your data was never in it.

In one minute

  • A role is a database login, and it holds privileges — the database's own list of who may SELECT, INSERT, UPDATE, DELETE which table. Your app uses one role, tasks, and it holds all of them.
  • Measured: REVOKE UPDATE, DELETE works even against the table's owner. I expected the owner to be immune. It is not — I checked instead of guessing.
  • Measured trap: TRUNCATE is a separate privilege. After revoking DELETE, one word still emptied the whole table.
  • Measured trap: the owner can GRANT it back in one line. So this guard stops a bug, not an attacker holding your database password.
  • Measured cost: npm test went 162 → 161 pass, 3 fail — and all 3 failures are test cleanup. Zero route handlers. Nothing your API does needs to delete an audit row.

The question this lesson asks

Lesson 36 gave you a rule: an audit row that vanishes with the person it accuses is not evidence. That is why AuditLog.userId has no @relation, so deleting a user cannot cascade the row away. Good. But now ask the next question:

DELETE FROM "AuditLog" WHERE "userId" = 7;

Who can run that line today? Your API can. It connects with DATABASE_URL, as the role tasks, and that role may do anything to any table. So the account that writes the evidence can also erase it. One SQL-injection hole, or one bad deleteMany in a future handler, and the log quietly loses the rows that mattered most.

role — a database login. Postgres calls it a role, not a user. Yours is tasks, created once by hand (the comment at the top of your .env).
privilege — one permission the database itself enforces: SELECT, INSERT, UPDATE, DELETE, TRUNCATE, on one table, for one role.
owner — the role that created the table. tasks owns every table in your database, because Prisma's migrations run as tasks.

This is the third costume of lesson 29's rule: a written rule is not enforcement. Your server/CLAUDE.md can say “never delete audit rows” forever. The database decides what is actually possible.

What I measured

I made a copy of your database (tasks_probe), ran your 14 migrations into it, and tried to lock the log down. Your real tasks database was read, never written, and the copy is deleted.

REVOKE UPDATE, DELETE, TRUNCATE ON "AuditLog" FROM tasks;
DELETE FROM "AuditLog" WHERE id = 1;
ERROR:  permission denied for table AuditLog

That surprised me. I assumed a table's owner could not be locked out of its own table. Wrong — owner privileges are granted by default, and anything granted can be revoked. Postgres stores the result in one column you can read:

Whenrelacl in pg_classMeans
beforeNULLnothing written down — the owner has everything
after{tasks=arxtm/tasks}a=INSERT, r=SELECT. No w (UPDATE), no d (DELETE), no D (TRUNCATE)

Then two things went wrong, and both are worth more than the success:

I triedWhat actually happened
REVOKE UPDATE, DELETE, then TRUNCATE probe;Table emptied. TRUNCATE is its own privilege. Revoking DELETE does not touch it.
GRANT DELETE ON probe TO tasks; as tasks itselfAllowed. The owner can hand the power back in one line, then delete.

So REVOKE against your own role is a guard against mistakes, not against an attacker. It stops a careless deleteMany, a copy-pasted cleanup script, and an injected single statement. It does not stop anyone who already holds your database password — they just grant it back. Say that limit out loud when you write it in the README; a guard you describe as stronger than it is, is worse than no guard.

Read that again, because it is the whole lesson. Your API never deletes an audit row. Only your tests do, to tidy up after themselves. The power that the tasks role holds over your log is used by exactly nobody in production code — and a power nobody uses is the cheapest one to take away.

One role is doing three jobs

The failing tests are not an argument against the lock. They are the lock telling you something true: the same login is doing three different jobs, and only one of them should be able to touch the log.

JobNeedsRuns when
serve requestsSELECT, INSERT, UPDATE, DELETE on normal tables. On AuditLog: SELECT and INSERT onlyalways, exposed to the internet
run migrationsCREATE, ALTER, DROP — the ownerat deploy, by you
clean up / keep the table smallDELETE on AuditLogtests, and one scheduled job later

The fix is not to give job 1 less code. It is to give job 1 its own role. This is least privilege: the login exposed to the internet holds only what serving a request actually needs. The other two jobs keep the owner role, and they run from your terminal, not from a web request.

least privilege — give each identity the smallest set of powers its job needs. You already apply this to people, in can(), since lesson 7. Today it applies to your app.

And a second role cannot grant itself anything back, because it owns nothing. That closes the hole the GRANT measurement opened.

Check yourself

Why is REVOKE DELETE from your own role only a partial guard?

Measured both ways. The revoke really works — the DELETE was refused with error 42501. But the owner still holds the right to change privileges on its own table, so one GRANT DELETE ON ... TO tasks; undoes it. It stops a mistake; it does not stop someone with the password.

What did the 3 failing tests actually prove?

Every failure came from a deleteMany in a test's cleanup. No route, no hook, no handler deletes an audit row. The privilege the app holds over its own log is used by nothing in production — which is why removing it costs nothing in production.

The three probes

tasks_probe — a throwaway copy of your database, deleted after
1. REVOKE UPDATE, DELETE, TRUNCATE ON "AuditLog" FROM tasks;
DELETE FROM "AuditLog" WHERE id = 1; ERROR: permission denied (42501)

SELECT count(*) FROM probe; 0 — the whole table, gone
 
3. GRANT DELETE ON probe TO tasks; GRANT

Probe 1 was the surprise: a table's owner can be revoked from its own table. Probes 2 and 3 are why that is not enough on its own.
