CORS: same-site vs same-origin

Your React app will be on a different port. Today you learn what the browser does about that — and why the fix everybody copies hands your users' sessions to strangers.

In one minute

  • A CORS error does not mean your server said no. Your server answered in full; the browser binned the reply.
  • Same-origin counts the port. Same-site does not. Two words, two jobs.
  • Anything that could change data gets a preflight OPTIONS first.
  • origin: true reflects whoever asked. With credentials: true that gives your sessions away.

This is why localhost development is confusing: two ports are cross-origin (so you need CORS) but same-site (so SameSite=Lax cookies still work).

The line that gives your sessions away

Never

cors({
  origin: true,        // echoes ANY origin
  credentials: true,   // ...with the cookie
})

Always

cors({
  origin: process.env.ALLOWED_ORIGINS.split(","),
  credentials: true,
})

origin: true means "reflect back whatever origin asked". So evil.com asks, gets told yes, and its script reads your logged-in data.

CORS is advice to a browser. It stops nobody with curl. Your access control is requireAuth + can(), and always was.

Check yourself

A CORS error appears in your console. What happened server-side?

The request arrived, the handler ran, the database was touched, the reply was sent. The browser then refused to hand it to the page. This is why CORS is not access control.

Do this

  1. npm test — 75 tests.
  2. curl -H 'Origin: https://evil.com' -i localhost:3000/tasks. Read the headers. Note you still got a reply — curl is not a browser.
  3. Homework: your API and your React app in production — how many ways can you arrange them? Write down three. Lesson 14 marks it.
